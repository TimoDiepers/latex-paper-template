# Writing Guide for Scientific Papers

Rules for writing a new paper and for reviewing an existing manuscript. Based on Weiss &
Newman (2011), internal LTT paper rules, and the "Sei Dein Eigener Lektor" presentation by
M. Engelpracht (see [Sources](#sources)).

Journal-specific requirements (word limits, section names, reference style) are not
covered here. Take those from the target journal's guide for authors.

**How to use this guide.** When drafting, follow Sections 2 to 10. When reviewing, work
through the checklist in Section 11, from coarse to fine.

## 1. Paper process

Plan before writing:

1. **Define the goal** of the work before starting.
2. **Idea sheet.** Develop the core idea, for example in seminars or group meetings.
   Iterate with the group leader, then ask the supervisor for feedback.
3. **Short outline.** Develop the paper structure and iterate the same way.
   *Decision point:* is this paper worth pursuing?
4. **Long outline.** A detailed outline with key figures, core findings and preliminary
   conclusions in their sections. Iterate, and agree on deadlines for writing and
   submission.
5. **Write the paper**, once the long outline is agreed.

Each step may take several iterations. Keep the cycles short.

## 2. Macro-structure (IMRaD)

| Section | Answers |
|---|---|
| **Abstract** | The full paper in short |
| **Introduction** | Why does this paper exist? |
| **Methodology** | How is the problem solved? |
| **Results** | What was found? |
| **Discussion** | What do the findings mean? |
| **Conclusions** | What is the central message, and what comes next? |

Every section after the introduction opens with a **topic sentence** that relates it to
what came before and gives direction.

## 3. Section-by-section guide

### 3.1 Abstract

The abstract is the most important part of the paper and often the only part that is
read. It must stand on its own. Build it sentence by sentence:

1. One sentence: an attention grabber that a **wide audience** understands.
2. One sentence: more detailed **background** for domain scientists.
3. One sentence: the **research question** or topic, clearly defined.
4. One sentence: the **main approach** and, if applicable, the hypothesis tested.
5. One sentence: the **main results**.
6. One or two sentences: **quantitative results** and what they mean compared with
   previous findings.
7. One or two sentences: **conclusions and recommendations** from a general perspective.

Rules:

- No equations, no references, and as few abbreviations as possible.
- Do not outline the paper's structure; summarize its essential content.
- Draft a preliminary abstract early to focus the writing, and revise it last.

### 3.2 Introduction

The introduction expands the first three sentences of the abstract. Move from
**general to specific** (a funnel):

1. **Engage the reader.** Establish the scientific context through seminal work, in
   chronological order.
2. **Narrow the focus.** Lead from the general to the specific and explain the knowledge
   gaps.
3. **Define the research question.** Link the gaps to a clear problem statement.
4. **Justify the approach.** Briefly name and justify the methods, scope and assumptions.
5. **Outline.** End with a short outline of the following sections.

### 3.3 Methodology

Explain, in logical order, how the research question is addressed:

1. **Define technical terms** that readers need for everything that follows.
2. **Explain the methods** from general to specific, in the order the analysis runs.
3. **Give examples** for complex approaches.
4. **Justify** the chosen method where several were possible.
5. **Make all calculations traceable**, so that peers can verify the results.

Rules:

- Focus on new methods and new data; cite standard approaches briefly.
- Give enough detail for others to repeat the work:
  - Established methods: name them and cite the original.
  - Newer methods: give more detail, and cite others who used them.
  - New methods: describe them in full.
- Move extensive input data or calculation procedures to an appendix.
- Reference every data source. Publish raw data as supplementary material.

### 3.4 Results

Present every finding needed to answer the research question:

1. Start **general**, end **specific**.
2. Point out important features, notable trends and relationships between results.
3. Let tables and figures **speak for themselves**.

Rules:

- Present most empirical findings in **diagrams**. Use tables only where exact values
  matter.
- Give quantitative **uncertainty** with the results.
- Do not:
  - present the same result in a table, a figure **and** the text;
  - describe methods (they belong in the methodology);
  - discuss at length (that belongs in the discussion).

### 3.5 Discussion

Interpret the results, in this order:

1. **Restate the principal results** in one or two sentences.
2. **Strengths and weaknesses** of the method, the input data and the results, with the
   sources of uncertainty.
3. **Compare** with other studies.
4. **Significance** relative to established knowledge.
5. **Open questions** and future research needs.

Rules:

- Follow the order of the results section.
- Never introduce new results in the discussion.
- Give an **unbiased** account, without unnecessary speculation.
- Do not inflate the importance of the results or conclude beyond what the evidence
  supports.
- Discuss how far the findings hold beyond the specific system studied.

### 3.6 Conclusions

The conclusions do not repeat the abstract. They:

1. **Answer the research question**, or state how far the knowledge gaps were closed.
2. Give the reader a **central message**.
3. Make **recommendations** and outline future research.

Open by briefly restating the problem and the approach: many readers read only the
abstract and the conclusions.

### 3.7 Supporting elements

- **Acknowledgements:** credit those who provided information, funding or review.
- **References:** collect them while writing, not at the end.
- **Appendices:** detailed methods or results that the main text does not need.

## 4. Paragraph structure

Each paragraph is a self-contained unit with three parts:

1. **Topic sentence:** states the main idea and gives direction.
2. **Supporting sentences:** explain, prove or elaborate the idea.
3. **Concluding sentence:** wraps up and leads into the next paragraph.

Rules:

- **Unity.** One topic per paragraph; every supporting sentence relates to it.
- **Coherence.** Each sentence follows logically and smoothly from the last:
  - repeat key nouns;
  - keep pronouns consistent (do not switch between "we", "one" and "they");
  - use transition signals.
- **Length.** Break after roughly 200 words.

### Paragraph types

| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| **Scientific argument** | Builds a case from evidence; known information in topic position, new in stress position |
| **Definition** | Relative clauses, necessary conditions, appositives, or contrast |
| **Description** | Systematic observations that enable replication |
| **Chronological** | Ordered by time adverbs, tense sequence or grammatical parallelism |
| **General to specific** | Background, common in introductions |
| **Problem to solution** | State the problem early, then solve it |
| **Cause and effect** | Logical arguments; cause before effect |
| **Comparison and contrast** | Compare in blocks; do not jump back and forth |

### Transition signals

| Purpose | Words |
|---|---|
| Addition | similarly, also, likewise, in comparison |
| Contrast | however, in contrast, whereas, conversely, unlike, while |
| Cause and effect | therefore, thus, consequently, accordingly, as a result |
| Example | for example, for instance, namely, specifically |
| Sequence | first, then, finally, subsequently, simultaneously |
| Problem to solution | but, however, nevertheless, nonetheless, instead, still, yet |

## 5. Structuring scientific arguments

### 5.1 Known information first, new information last

Readers expect **known information at the start** of a sentence (topic position) and
**new information at the end** (stress position). The principle holds at every level:
article, section, paragraph and sentence. Each sentence should link back to its
predecessor.

- Bad: "Disruptive transformations in all energy systems are necessary for tapping widely
  available renewable energy resources."
- Good: "Tapping widely available renewable energy resources requires, however,
  disruptive transformations in all energy systems."

### 5.2 Use verbs that name the action

Replace vague verbs ("is", "are") with specific ones ("differ", "define", "introduce").
Prefer associated statements ("we analyze") to dissociated ones ("we perform an
analysis").

### 5.3 Begin with the main clause

Readers expect the principal agent as the grammatical subject, at the start.

- Bad: "In economic-energy-environmental modeling approaches, the representation of
  technological changes is one of the most important sources of uncertainty."
- Good: "The representation of technological changes in economic-energy-environmental
  models introduces uncertainty into estimates of the economic costs."

### 5.4 Keep subject and verb close together

- Bad: "Biofuel production, especially in countries endowed with land and other natural
  resources, favourable weather conditions and abundant labour, can be a means of
  promoting agriculture."
- Good: "Biofuel production can be a means of promoting agriculture and rural
  development, especially in countries endowed with land, other natural resources,
  favorable weather conditions, and abundant labor."

### 5.5 Make every reference unambiguous

- Each pronoun must point to exactly one subject or object.
- **Never use "this" without a noun.** Write "This assumption…", "This result…",
  "This approach…".
- Replace "it" and "this" with the noun itself wherever possible.

### 5.6 Keep related information together

State related pieces of information in the same place, particularly in two-clause
sentences, rather than splitting them across the paragraph.

### 5.7 Use parallel structure for parallel ideas

Similar form lets the reader recognize similar content.

- Bad: "At present wind power supplies 15% of the Danish electricity demand and ca. 50%
  is produced in CHP."
- Good: "At present, wind power supplies 15% and CHP supplies 50% of the Danish
  electricity demand."

### 5.8 Complete compound constructions

Include every word a compound construction needs, especially prepositions, and every
word a comparison with "than" needs.

- Bad: "The model depends and is calibrated on field data."
- Good: "The model depends on and is calibrated against field data."
- Bad: "The efficiency of the new plant is higher than the old plant."
- Good: "The efficiency of the new plant is higher than that of the old plant."

## 6. Sentence-level writing

### 6.1 Write in the active voice

Active voice is more direct, and it separates the authors' work from other research.

- Bad: "Data for the supply chain has been collected reaching back three decades."
- Good: "We have collected data for the supply chain reaching back three decades."

Use the passive only when the sentence is about the object, or the agent is unimportant
or unknown.

### 6.2 Use the editorial "we"

Use "we" even in single-author papers. It avoids overemphasizing one person, reads more
naturally, and avoids the passive.

### 6.3 Write short sentences

Prefer short, clear sentences. Put a subordinate clause **after** the main clause.

- Bad: "Because of the high temperatures, we assume a linear relationship."
- Good: "We assume a linear relationship because of the high temperatures."

### 6.4 State things positively

Readers want to know what **is**, not what is not.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| do not account for | exclude, neglect |
| do not allow | prevent |
| not always straightforward | complicated, difficult |
| not clear | unclear, unresolved |
| not constant | alternating, changing |
| not economically viable | uneconomical, unprofitable |
| not important | unimportant, negligible |
| not included in | excluded from |
| not feasible | infeasible |
| not sufficiently reliable | unreliable |

### 6.5 Avoid nominalizations

Nominalizations turn verbs and adjectives into nouns and blur the action.

- Bad: "Many studies have been carried out on the subject of biological hydrogen
  production."
- Good: "Biological hydrogen production has been studied frequently."

Check "there is / there are" constructions in particular:

- Bad: "There is a growing trend towards distributed electricity production."
- Good: "Distributed electricity production is growing."

### 6.6 Cut wordiness

Every word must earn its place.

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| a considerable amount of | much, many |
| as a consequence of | because |
| in order to | to |
| the fact that | because, although |
| through the use of | by, with |
| it is worth pointing out that | note that |
| regardless of the fact that | even though |
| on the basis of | by |
| one of the most important | an important |
| would seem to indicate | indicates |
| during the course of | while |
| at a rapid rate | rapidly |

Rephrase anything built on "the fact that", "one of the most" or "there are … which
are …". Delete sentences starting with "Needless to say". Instead of writing "It is
interesting that…", make the statement itself interesting.

### 6.7 Avoid subjective statements

Leave out opinions and value judgments. Replace subjective words with objective ones
("better" → "higher").

### 6.8 Hedge where claims are uncertain

- **Verbs:** appear, seem, suggest, indicate, estimate, tend
- **Adverbs:** approximately, arguably, relatively, typically, slightly, fairly
- **Modals:** can, may, might, could, likely, unlikely
- **Expressions:** on balance, as a rule, in principle, to some extent

Hedge only as much as the evidence requires; do not hedge established results.

### 6.9 Emphasize deliberately

Structures for stressing a point:

- *It* + *be* + relative clause: "It is the heat loss, not the pressure drop, that limits
  the efficiency."
- *What*-clause + *be*: "What limits the efficiency is the heat loss."
- Inversion after a negative or limiting word: "Only at full load does the process reach
  steady state."
- Emphatic adverb after *be* or *have* and before other verbs: "The model does capture the
  peak", "The results clearly show…".

## 7. Word choice

### 7.1 Consistent terminology

- Give each thing **one name** and use it throughout. No synonyms for technical terms.
- Do not alternate adjectives such as "entire", "total" and "absolute" in an established
  phrase.
- Do not use nouns such as "use", "consumption" and "demand" interchangeably.

### 7.2 Specific words

- **"In particular"**, not "especially".
- **"Therefore"** means "hence" or "consequently", not "for this purpose".
- **"Boundary condition"** only for differential equations; otherwise "specification" or
  similar.
- **"Different"** is almost always filler. Test: replace it with "same". If the sentence
  then makes no sense, "different" was superfluous.
- **"Must"** is rarely right in English; it expresses a very strong obligation.
- Prefer **verbs to noun stacks**: not "global warming impact reductions" but "reduce
  global warming impacts".
- Do not use **abbreviations as nouns** in running text (do not shorten "global warming
  impact" to "GWI").

### 7.3 Do not rely on dictionary translations

When unsure how a word is used, do not look it up in a general bilingual dictionary.
Look for the term in good papers on the same topic.

### 7.4 Commonly confused words

| Word | Meaning | Not to be confused with |
|---|---|---|
| accept (v.) | to agree to receive | except (prep.): not including |
| advise (v.) | to recommend | advice (n.): a recommendation |
| affect (v.) | to change, to influence | effect (n.): a result |
| among (prep.) | within a group of three or more | between (prep.): two things |
| continual | happening repeatedly | continuous: without interruption |
| rise (v., intransitive) | to move upward | raise (v., transitive): to lift or increase something |
| extent (n.) | the degree to which something applies | extend (v.): to stretch, to enlarge |
| due to | "caused by"; follows a form of *be* ("The delay is due to…") | because of: modifies a verb ("It failed because of…") |

## 8. Grammar and punctuation

### 8.1 Tenses

Write in the **present tense** throughout. It limits ambiguity and keeps the reader
focused.

An accepted alternative: present tense for established knowledge and for references to
tables and figures, past tense for your own methods and results. Pick one convention and
keep it.

Either way:

- No future tense: not "In Section 5, we will show…" (Section 5 already exists), but
  "Section 5 shows…".
- No present progressive.

### 8.2 Commas

- Use commas generously where they help reading, particularly after introductory
  phrases: "In summary, …".
- **No comma before "that"**: "He showed that…", not "He showed, that…".
- Defining relative clauses take no commas; non-defining ones do (Section 8.5).
- When a sentence ends in a parenthesis, the full stop goes **outside** it (like this).

### 8.3 Subject–verb agreement

- "And" takes a plural verb.
- "Or" and "nor" take a singular verb. When they join a singular and a plural subject,
  the verb agrees with the nearer one.

### 8.4 Parenthetical expressions

Enclose them in commas or parentheses. Better, avoid them: they interrupt the reasoning.

### 8.5 Relative clauses: "that" and "which"

- **Defining** (restrictive) clause: singles out one object among similar ones. No commas.
  Use "that". ("The model that includes storage converges faster.")
- **Non-defining** clause: adds a fact about an object already identified. Set off with
  commas. Use "which". ("The model, which includes storage, converges faster.")

### 8.6 Compound adjectives

Hyphenate words that together modify a noun: "model-based experiments",
"high-temperature heat".

### 8.7 Spelling

- Choose UK or US English and keep it throughout.
- "Cannot" is one word.
- "Its" is possessive; "it's" means "it is" (and does not belong in a paper).

## 9. Figures and tables

### 9.1 In the text

- Reference every table and figure in the text **before** it appears.
- Refer to figures and tables **parenthetically**, and let the sentence carry the
  content. Not "Our model is shown in Fig. 2." but "Our model couples the heat pump to
  thermal storage (Fig. 2)." In LaTeX: `(Fig.~\ref{fig:model})`.
- Do not present the same result in a table, a figure and the text.
- Present most empirical findings in **diagrams**; use tables only where exact values
  matter.

### 9.2 Readability

- Figures must be **readable in black and white**.
- No titles or headings inside figures; the caption does that job.

### 9.3 Captions

- Describe **everything** visible in the figure.
- The reader must understand the figure **without the main text**.
- Captions may run to several sentences.

### 9.4 Variables and symbols

- Name a variable together with its symbol: "The diffusion coefficient *D* is defined
  by…".
- Choose meaningful subscripts: *T*<sub>hot</sub> and *T*<sub>cold</sub>, not
  *T*<sub>1</sub> and *T*<sub>2</sub>.

## 10. Formatting and conventions

### 10.1 Title and keywords

Both must represent the content precisely, so that readers grasp the topic at a glance
and find the article in databases and search engines.

### 10.2 Units and abbreviations

- Use **SI units**. Spell out a unit at first use, in lowercase ("kelvin"), even where its
  symbol is uppercase (K). In LaTeX, set quantities with `siunitx`: `\qty{300}{\kelvin}`.
- Define **every abbreviation** at first use, including country codes and currencies.
- Avoid abbreviations the general readership would not know.
- Abbreviate countries with **two-letter ISO codes** in tables and figures.
- Denote money with **three-letter ISO currency codes**, and state whether values are
  current or constant (with the base year).

### 10.3 Numbers and precision

- Round numbers to **significant digits**.
- Make **quantitative** statements rather than qualitative ones: readers need to know
  "by how much", not just "larger".

### 10.4 References

- Reference each scientific statement individually.
- Cite **primary sources**, not secondary literature, so readers can find the origin.
- Add references to `references.bib` as you write, not at the end.

### 10.5 Style

- Avoid qualifiers such as "rather", "very" and "little"; they dilute the message.
- Do not interrupt the reasoning with footnotes or heavy cross-referencing.
- Write each paper **fresh**. Do not reuse sentences or passages from earlier papers.
- Use a **colon** to join a main clause to its elaboration: "The results indicate a clear
  trend: Higher temperatures lead to faster degradation."

## 11. Revision checklist

Use this when reviewing your own or someone else's manuscript. Work **from coarse to
fine**: fix structure before sentences, and sentences before commas.

### Pass 1: Structure

- [ ] Does the paper follow IMRaD?
- [ ] Does each section contain the elements listed in Section 3?
- [ ] Does the abstract follow the seven-element template?
- [ ] Does the introduction move from general to specific and end with a clear research
      question?
- [ ] Does the methodology allow replication?
- [ ] Does the results section present findings without discussing them?
- [ ] Does the discussion interpret results without introducing new ones?
- [ ] Do the conclusions answer the research question and give a central message?
- [ ] Does each paragraph cover one idea, with a topic sentence, supporting sentences and
      a concluding sentence?
- [ ] Are transitions between paragraphs smooth and logical?
- [ ] Does information flow from known to new?

### Pass 2: Content and expression

- [ ] Are sentences short and clear?
- [ ] Are uninformative words deleted?
- [ ] Are nominalizations replaced with verbs?
- [ ] Are statements positive, without needless negation?
- [ ] Do parallel ideas have parallel structure?
- [ ] Are compound constructions and comparisons complete?
- [ ] Are subjects and verbs close together?
- [ ] Does each sentence start with known information and end with new information?
- [ ] Are the verbs specific rather than "be" or "have"?
- [ ] Is the active voice used consistently?
- [ ] Is hedging used where, and only where, claims are uncertain?
- [ ] Are subjective statements and value judgments gone?
- [ ] Is every "this" followed by a noun?

### Pass 3: Language and grammar

- [ ] Is the tense convention consistent (preferably present throughout)?
- [ ] Is there no future tense and no present progressive?
- [ ] Do subjects and verbs agree?
- [ ] Are commas right, in particular none before "that" and commas around non-defining
      clauses?
- [ ] Is one variety of English (UK or US) used throughout?
- [ ] Are compound adjectives hyphenated?
- [ ] Is every abbreviation defined at first use?
- [ ] Is terminology consistent, with no synonyms for technical terms?
- [ ] Are parenthetical expressions properly enclosed, or removed?

### Pass 4: Figures, tables and formatting

- [ ] Is every figure and table referenced in the text before it appears?
- [ ] Are figure references parenthetical, with the sentence carrying the content?
- [ ] Are captions self-contained and complete?
- [ ] Are figures readable in black and white?
- [ ] Is no result presented redundantly in a table, a figure and the text?
- [ ] Are SI units used throughout?
- [ ] Are numbers rounded to significant digits?
- [ ] Are statements quantitative rather than qualitative?
- [ ] Are variables named together with their symbols?
- [ ] Is every reference a primary source, and is every statement that needs one
      referenced?
